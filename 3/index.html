Fun with Flow Models

Part A: denoising, sampling and editing images with a pretrained flow model. Part B: flow matching from scratch.

Sneha Menon · Intro to Computer Vision & Computational Photography

1. Visualizing Training and Sampling

In flow matching, we utilize optimal-transport (OT) linear interpolation between standard Gaussian noise $x_0 = \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and a clean image $x_1$ across timestep parameter $t \in [0, 1]$:

$$x_t = (1 - t)x_0 + tx_1, \quad t \in [0, 1]$$

Here, $t = 0$ represents pure noise, while $t = 1$ corresponds to the clean target image. Smaller values of $t$ indicate higher levels of noise.

1.1 Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

To construct noisy inputs, we sample independent Gaussian noise $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and interpolate it with a clean photograph center-cropped and resized to $512 \times 512$ with RGB values scaled to $[-1, 1]$. A single noise tensor is sampled once and reused across all timesteps $t \in \{0.25, 0.50, 0.75\}$ to ensure direct comparability.

$$x_t = (1 - t)\epsilon + tx_1, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ 
def interpolate(image, noise, t):
    return (1 - t) * noise + t * image

Discussion: As seen above, reusing the same sampled noise tensor highlights how increasing $t$ preserves progressively more structural information from the original image. At $t = 0.25$, high-frequency details are heavily obscured by noise variance, whereas by $t = 0.75$, the primary architectural geometry of the building is clearly visible.
