Image Warping and Mosaicing

Part A: recovering homographies, inverse warping, rectification, and blended photo mosaics.

Sneha Menon · Intro to Computer Vision & Computational Photography

A.1 Shooting the Pictures

I shot two sets of overlapping photos, standing in one spot and rotating the camera between shots so the center of projection stayed (roughly) fixed. Under a pure rotation, the images are related by a homography. I kept adjacent photos at roughly 40–70% overlap and kept the near foreground out of the overlap where possible, since close objects shift (parallax) and no single homography can align them.

A.2 Recovering Homographies

A homography $H$ maps a point $(x, y)$ in image 1 to $(x', y')$ in image 2. In homogeneous coordinates, $[x', y', 1]^T \sim H\,[x, y, 1]^T$, and since scale does not matter I fix the bottom-right entry to $1$, leaving $8$ unknowns $(a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h)$:

$$x' = \frac{ax + by + c}{gx + hy + 1}, \qquad y' = \frac{dx + ey + f}{gx + hy + 1}$$

Multiplying out the denominator makes both equations linear in the unknowns, so every correspondence contributes two rows of $A\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{b}$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x x' & -y x' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -x y' & -y y' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix}$$

Four points give exactly 8 equations, which fits any click error perfectly and makes $H$ unstable. I used 10 correspondences per image pair (20 equations) and solved the overdetermined system with least squares (np.linalg.lstsq). I also normalize the points (zero mean, mean distance $\sqrt{2}$) before solving and undo the normalization afterward, which improves numerical conditioning without changing the solution. 

def computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts, normalize=True):
    p1n, T1 = _normalize_pts(im1_pts)
    p2n, T2 = _normalize_pts(im2_pts)
    A, b = build_system(p1n, p2n)               # two rows per correspondence
    h = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)[0]    # least squares
    Hn = np.append(h, 1.0).reshape(3, 3)
    H = np.linalg.inv(T2) @ Hn @ T1             # undo normalization
    return H / H[2, 2]

A.3 Warping the Images

I warp with inverse warping: the output bounding box comes from sending the four corners of the input through $H$, and for every output pixel I compute where it came from with $H^{-1}$ and sample the source image there. Forward warping would leave holes wherever $H$ stretches the image; inverse warping gives every output pixel exactly one value. Output pixels whose source falls outside the image get value $0$ and are marked invalid in an alpha mask, which the mosaic step uses later.

I implemented both interpolation methods from scratch, with the integer coordinate denoting the center of a pixel:

  • Nearest neighbor: round the source coordinate to the closest pixel and copy its value.
  • Bilinear: take the four surrounding pixels and weight each by the area of the sub-rectangle opposite to it, with $f_x, f_y$ the fractional parts:
$$I(x,y) = (1-f_x)(1-f_y)I_{00} + f_x(1-f_y)I_{10} + (1-f_x)f_y I_{01} + f_x f_y I_{11}$$

Quality vs. speed: Nearest neighbor does one lookup per output pixel, so it is the faster of the two, but it turns edges and text into jagged stair-steps. Bilinear does four lookups plus the weights for every pixel, so it takes roughly 2.5–2.7× as long, but it produces smooth edges and is only slightly softer. The difference is only tens of milliseconds, so I used bilinear for all the mosaics, where jagged edges would show up in every overlap.

A.4 Blending Images into a Mosaic

My procedure for each mosaic of three photos:

  1. Compute a homography between each adjacent pair of images. The middle image is the reference and stays unwarped: the left image is warped into its frame with $H_{12}$, and the right image with $H_{23}^{-1}$.
  2. Send every image's four corners through its homography and take the union bounding box, which gives one shared canvas size.
  3. Inverse-warp each image (bilinear) onto its own canvas of that size, along with a weight map equal to the distance to the nearest image edge (normalized to 1 at the center, like bwdist), warped as an extra channel.
  4. Blend. I compared three methods: overwrite (hard seams), feathering (weighted average, $\sum w_i I_i / \sum w_i$), and two-band blending.

Two-band blending is a two-level Laplacian pyramid. Each image is split into a low band (Gaussian blur) and a high band (image minus blur). The low bands are feathered, so color and brightness fade smoothly across the overlap. The high bands are taken from whichever image has the largest weight at each pixel, so edges are never averaged and do not ghost. The blur is computed only over each image's own valid pixels so that the black border does not darken the edges.
